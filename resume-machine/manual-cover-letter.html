Open source aligns with my values. Automattic's creed to show respect and empathy mirrors that practice; a culture of mutual support enables honest feedback, thoughtful collaboration, and real shared ownership.

AI should amplify human creativity. I want to work on technology that enhances people's capabilities rather than replaces their judgment. Automattic's measured adoption of large language models suggests thoughtful stewardship of these tools, and that clarity makes now the right time for me to contribute.

Which part of the Automattic Creed resonates most with you, and why?

"I am more motivated by impact than money, and I know that Open Source is one of the most powerful ideas of our generation."

Open source resonates most with me because it expands capacity, grants ownership, and preserves choice for users.

Open source expands capacity for the underfunded. Community-maintained tools let small organizations access infrastructure they could not otherwise afford.

Access matters, but ownership matters too. Open source gives users the ability to inspect, modify, and control their tools, so software becomes an asset rather than a rented service.

Ownership preserves choice. When code is open, organizations can switch providers, avoid lock-in, and resist opaque pricing or extractive licensing.

Because of these principles, I oppose an extraction-first ethos. Sustainable impact comes from empowering users, not from maximizing short-term revenue per client.

Tell us about a project or piece of work you're especially proud of.

At GOOP, I led the rollout of a self-service CMS that removed a persistent bottleneck between Editorial and Engineering. When I arrived, editors were designing posts in Figma and Engineering was rebuilding each design by hand. That workflow was slow, costly, and left no room for higher-impact work.

Earlier attempts to introduce self-service tools had failed because editors felt excluded and the tools didn't match their process. To avoid the same mistake, I spoke with staff across departments and listened for their real needs and constraints.

We implemented a component-based system built on ACF Flexible Content. The components give editors clear, simple controls while preserving the design flexibility they need. Behind the scenes, the implementation uses pragmatic WordPress/PHP patterns and modular editor components, so the codebase stays maintainable.

Within three months, the editorial team had adopted the system and the Figma-to-code handoff had disappeared. Engineering reclaimed most of the time previously spent on article builds and could focus on higher-value projects. The change made the content workflow faster, more reliable, and easier to maintain.

What's the most recent idea, book, blog post, or talk that changed how you think about your craft?

I recently read "Sleep-time Compute: Beyond Inference Scaling at Test-time" (Kevin Lin et al., arXiv:2504.13171v1). The paper proposes precomputing reasoning over persistent context during idle periods so that queries can be answered with less test-time compute and lower latency.

The approach improves accuracy at query time. By doing heavy reasoning during idle periods, the system delivers better answers when a query arrives.

It also cuts real-time compute and cost. Shifting work offline reduces test-time tokens and can lower demand for hardware, energy, and water.

Beyond technical gains, the approach supports healthier work rhythms. If expensive computation can happen while systems are idle, engineers and users face less pressure to run long, intense sessions.

Finally, it resonated with me personally. Stepping away from a problem often lets subconscious processes work in the background. Sleep-time compute mirrors that habit and gives it a concrete, technical justification.

I am actually completing a home-lab PC build that I will be using as a self-hosted LLM (Large Language Model). One of my next projects will be to set up sleep-time computing! This setup allows me to utilize the computational resources efficiently during off-peak hours, ensuring that I can run complex tasks without impacting daily performance.

What's a controversial or unpopular opinion you hold about software engineering?

Documentation is a crucial feature of a healthy repository.

This is not a radical idea on paper, but in practice documentation is often neglected. Teams inherit outdated guides, documentation goes unused, or writing docs is treated as a chore. Good documentation requires time and attention, and people become cynical when that work isn't valued.

Today, neglect is harder to excuse. Tools like phpDocumentor and TypeDoc, paired with agentic AI, make generating and maintaining accurate docs far easier than before.

Documentation is leverage, not bureaucracy. Clear docs speed onboarding, reduce context switching, and make pair-programming with LLMs far more effective. They preserve knowledge through staff changes and make codebases more resilient.

I treat documentation as a product feature: I document intent, examples, and maintenance notes, and I invest in automation and review so that docs stay accurate and discoverable.

Tell us about a time you strongly disagreed with someone on your team.

My current employer used WordPress for content management for over a decade. Over time, they hired software engineers focused primarily on React and Node.js; nobody knew PHP or was a WordPress expert. As a result, engineers often created convoluted, fragile PHP infrastructure—developing shims and shortcuts to meet short-term acceptance criteria at the expense of long-term stability and scalability.

I was hired in 2022 to develop a viable content builder the editorial team would adopt and to rein in the chaos from the previous decade. I achieved the first objective and was making steady progress on the second. At the end of 2025, the team had an opportunity to reboot the CMS.

“Ah ha,” I thought. With a fresh WordPress installation, I could quickly eliminate major friction points. Leadership, however, wanted to consider new and “modern” CMS options. I articulated my position:

Sticking with WordPress had advantages:

  • The editorial team already knew it well.
  • It’s a mature platform with strong long-term support.
  • No database migration would be required.
  • With a clean theme, we could implement efficient business logic the whole team could understand and finally pivot our ACF-based builder from Classic to Block Editor, which was a longstanding goal.

Migrating carried risks:

  • The editorial team would need to retrain and might slow down during the transition.
  • A younger CMS might not endure, leading to another replatform.
  • Data migration would be costly and complex due to deprecated interfaces.

Effectively, I stood alone. The primary argument for replatforming was that the company would benefit from a CMS aligned with the engineering team’s core stack.

I kept the situation in perspective. While I had a clear preference, moving away from WordPress would not spell doom for the organization. I explained my reasoning and engaged thoughtfully with opposing views. When the decision was made to move to Payload, I embraced it fully. In the end, we work as a team to identify the best solution for our stakeholders and clients, even when it’s not our preferred path.
