## **Entrepreneurial Journey: Building Boiling Pot Media (2011-2021)**

### **The Beginning: Solo Founder (2011-2013)**

In 2011, while still working in community development, James founded Boiling Pot Media, a digital agency that would become his primary focus and greatest learning ground for the next decade. He started as a solo operation, handling every aspect of the business: client acquisition, contracts, design, development, and VPS management.

The technical foundation he built during this period was comprehensive. As the agency grew, James faced the challenge every founder encounters: how to scale beyond his own capacity. He began hiring, eventually building a team of six people including a content writer, graphic designer, administrator, and two additional software engineers beyond himself.

This was James's first experience building and managing an engineering team. He established coding standards, implemented code review processes, and created project workflows. ### **Technical Achievements & Scale**

During the decade at Boiling Pot Media, James and his team delivered 125+ website projects for clients. Several of these clients already had substantial traffic volumes, and the websites James built had to support and optimize for that scale. His most significant technical achievement was scaling infrastructure and performance for high-traffic client websites, with some platforms handling over 1 million daily visits.

### **The Strategic Exit (2021)**

By 2021, Boiling Pot Media had reached its most profitable year. However, James had come to a crucial realization: despite the business success, he found himself stretched too thin across too many roles. As he reflected, "As a founder, I found myself stretched between very many roles (manager, owner, lead sales person, lead software engineer, customer support/contact, accountant, legal, team recruiter...)."

He faced a decision: continue growing a successful but demanding business, or refocus on his areas of greatest talent and passion.

James chose deliberately. The decision to leave a profitable business to focus on technical leadership demonstrated self-awareness and conviction about his professional identity.

When he arrived, the merchandising process was entirely manual and consuming 10-25% of each workday (roughly 1-2 hours). The impact was dramatic: product and inventory management time dropped from 10-25% of the day to just 5%, reclaiming valuable engineering capacity for high-impact projects. ### **Transformative Achievement: The Self-Service CMS**

Perhaps James's most impactful contribution at GOOP was solving a problem that had plagued the organization for years: the bottleneck between Editorial and Engineering.

#### **The Problem Context**

When James joined GOOP, there were three team members on the "web team": one project manager who could edit WordPress posts, and two software engineers. Remarkably, 95-100% of their time was spent hard-coding custom-designed WordPress posts based on Figma designs provided by the Editorial team. Every article required bespoke engineering work.

Previous attempts to create a CMS experience for the Editorial team had failed. The tools lacked sufficient flexibility in how content could be rendered, and the Editorial team had low adoption appetite—they were used to having complete design control via Figma, and prior CMS solutions felt limiting.

#### **Solution**

James approached this differently. He conducted stakeholder interviews to understand the Editorial team's actual needs and pain points. He then architected a flexible React-based component system for WordPress that balanced two competing requirements: enough flexibility to satisfy Editorial's design standards, and enough structure to be truly self-service.

The system he created allowed non-technical writers to create custom content layouts using pre-built components. The Editorial team could achieve their design goals without Figma mockups and without engineering intervention.

#### **The Impact**

The adoption was remarkable—100% of the Editorial team embraced the new system within three months. The Editorial team eliminated Figga designs from their workflow entirely, saving significant time in their own process. The system became genuinely self-service.

For the engineering team, the impact was dramatic. The web team project manager role was eliminated (no longer needed), and the two engineers were freed to work on high-value projects. James estimates this reclaimed 97% of engineering capacity previously consumed by custom article creation. The Editorial team now publishes approximately three times more content with the same headcount.

As his manager wrote in his 2024 annual review: "I believe that James's responsiveness to the editorial team greatly contributes to company success. When issues come up, he is quick to respond. This forms goodwill between the Editorial Team and the Tech Team, as they know that their issues are taken seriously."

#### **Sales Narrative**

Tell us about a technical sale that you won (or helped win) that involved you delivering a custom or complex demo.

Raybend, a sump-pump manufacturer, inquired about hosting. The company that had created their WordPress website was no longer available.

Their website was simple—a limited frontend for brand information and a basic pump serial number tracking plugin. They gave me administrator access so I could review the setup before I visited.

When I arrived for our on-site meeting, I was greeted by two stakeholders: Joe—owner and no-nonsense businessperson—and Dagne, his right hand. During introductions I noted that I may have installed some of Raybend's pumps in the past. I worked with my dad \- a master plumber \- often before and throughout college. I have a wide range of professional experience and find sharing that information makes clients more likely share a detailed account of their work. People who swing a hammer like to know that you can swing a hammer, too. After an impatient handshake, Joe left Dagne to bring me on a tour of the facility. Dagne did a little damage control, explaining that Joe's head was always in two places at once.

Dagne walked me through their back office, engineering room, and manufacturing floor, explaining how Raybend stood apart from their competition by physically testing each pump against predefined performance criteria. Pumps that fail don't get sold.

During the floor tour, I asked questions. What was the significance of the PSI and amperage range tests? How did operators know if a pump passed or failed? What did "pump head" mean? Dagne had a lot of technical knowledge to impart. I asked how much time it took to assemble a unit, and Dagne illustrated common points of friction for the assembly line. At least weekly, she or Joe would run a stopwatch to benchmark assembly speed. I remarked that when I worked at a commercial greenhouse, the owner would run a stopwatch on the assembly line, too. But if we knew there was a stopwatch on the floor, we were going to double our effort to avoid getting in trouble so he could never get a true measurement of throughput.

My impression is that Dagne observed and appreciated the combination of focus and curiosity I brought to understanding their operation.

Dagne shared that they had contacted several other companies in the region that had expressed little interest in Raybend. I imagined disinterested voices over tinny phone lines saying, "Yeah, sure Dagne, your website isn't a big deal, we can host it no problem."

Joe wanted a contact who understood his company, she said. I had requested to visit—I was curious. I showed up on time—I kept a commitment. Joe liked those things.

Our tour ended at Joe's office. It was apparent they had decided to give me the hosting contract. I had provided a clear sales sheet on my service and commitment. Dagne had asked several questions on the tour—mostly about disaster recovery—and gave Joe a CliffsNotes review.

Joe wasn't interested in talking about hosting anymore. He appeared content that I had satisfied Dagne. Half his attention was spent deleting emails.

I had saved some thoughts that I wanted to share with both of them present.

First, I suggested that they leverage the existing plugin data to calculate the average time it took to assemble a pump. The serial number tracking system was a custom post type with a custom field. Calculating pumps-per-hour was as simple as exporting the post data and setting up a spreadsheet computation. Of course, that kind of reporting feature could also be built into a dashboard on the website.

The second suggestion was to configure the serial number field to automatically fill the next serial number. Operators were typing ten-digit numbers with leading zeros ("0000069218"), which looked time consuming. "And error prone," Dagne added. She explained that when errors occurred—duplicate serial numbers, transposed digits—she or another office staff member would need to log into WordPress, find the errors, and manually correct them. It was a massive time-saver she was describing, which always equates to money.

Joe wasn't multitasking anymore. What they disclosed next indicated that Joe believed I might have the skillset to help him complete a vision he'd set aside. The previous developer had not impressed Joe, and he'd shelved his ideas, unclear on who could build what he really wanted.

The feature he envisioned would track unique performance metrics for each pump, the line operator who entered the data, retail price, warranty expiration date, produce QR code labels, feature several user roles, and offer the ability—without calling in a software engineer—to configure new metrics for additional pump models over time.

I outlined how I would approach the project using WordPress post types, taxonomies, and custom fields to create a robust unit tracking system. When they asked about comprehensive tracking capabilities, I explained how these tools could scale to meet their needs.

Two weeks later, I brought my laptop to our next meeting and demoed bootstrapped pages. I had sent Dagne two messages during that time to let her know I was on track. #### **Technical Overview**

This is a comprehensive WordPress-based quality control system for manufacturing pump products. It combines PHP backend logic with JavaScript validation to create a sophisticated data entry, storage, and retrieval platform.

#### **Core Functionality**

##### **1\. Product Testing & Data Entry**

The system implements a multi-layered validation framework for quality assurance testing:

* **Dynamic Form Generation**: PHP generates testing forms based on product model specifications stored in WordPress taxonomy metadata
* **Real-time Validation**: jQuery validation enforces acceptable ranges for:
  * Discharge Pressure (PSI) at three test points
  * Current (AMPS) at three test points
* **Serial Number Tracking**: Prevents duplicate entries by comparing against the last submitted serial number within the session
* **Conditional Validation**: Models can have validations enabled/disabled based on configuration

##### **2\. Session Management & Workflow Optimization**

The system employs sophisticated session handling to streamline operator workflow:

* **20-Minute Session Windows**: Auto-resets if no activity occurs
* **Model Memory**: Automatically re-selects the last tested product model
* **Serial Number Continuity**: Tracks the previous serial number to prevent operator errors
* **User Context Preservation**: Maintains operator identity throughout the session

##### **3\. Data Storage Architecture**

Custom Post Type System:

* Each test creates a `product_record` post with status of either 'publish' (approved) or 'void'
* Stores six performance metrics as post metadata
* Links to product model via custom taxonomy

Product Model Configuration:

* Stores validation ranges (min/max values for each test parameter)
* Maintains serial number sequences
* Contains design specifications and graphing parameters
* Tracks pricing history with JSON serialization
* Configurable display options (hide from dropdowns, disable charts)

##### **4\. Barcode Generation & Label Printing**

* Generates CODE128 barcodes using JsBarcode library
* Combines line ID with serial number
* Opens print dialog automatically (unless disabled per model)
* Error logging for print failures

##### **5\. Product Lookup & Retrieval**

* Custom search functionality by serial number
* Displays test results, operator initials, and test date
* Links to full product records
* Handles partial matches and multiple results

##### **6\. Data Visualization**

The system includes configuration for performance charting:

* FT Factor calculations
* Pipe size cross-sectional area settings
* Design specification performance data storage
* Chart.js integration for visual representation
